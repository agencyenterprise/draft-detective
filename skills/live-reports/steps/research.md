# Step 3: Research one item

You research a single item of the research plan: one fact to check, one data source to update, or one angle to explore. Others are researching the other items, so stay on yours. Report what changed in the world; don't judge what it means for the report's conclusions, which is the brief's job.

You are given the item, the report's title, the cutoff that applies to your item, the plan's source guidance and the path for your notes. A report can have two cutoffs: its publication date, and an earlier date when its data ends. The plan says which one applies to which items.

## What you are looking for

**What has happened since the cutoff, up to today.** The report already shows what was true when it was written; confirming that adds nothing. Your question is whether the item is still true now, and what changed between the cutoff and today. Every finding you report must rest on sources published after the cutoff. Sources from before it can explain the background, never settle the verdict.

## How to research

1. **Know what you are looking for.**
   - **A fact:** decide what would have to be true today for it to still hold, and what would show it no longer does: a new law or policy, a reorganization, newer data, a later study.
   - **A fact the authors inferred from their data** (the plan says "From: inference over D…"): you can't rerun their analysis. Look for newer evidence on the same question: a newer release of the same data, or other studies that measured it since. Report what you find, and say that the authors' own estimate would need their analysis rerun. Don't recompute it yourself: if the fact is stated in the authors' own measure (an estimate from their model, a scale or index of theirs), never convert other sources' figures into it. Report those figures as their sources give them.
   - **A data source:** find the newest release of the same series or dataset, from the same publisher. Note what changed in it: the period it covers, its figures for what the report used, and any change of method or definitions that makes the figures hard to compare.
   - **An angle:** decide what a significant development since the cutoff would look like.
2. **Search the years since the cutoff.** Try several queries with different terms, including the official names of laws, programs and agencies, and include recent years or "since <cutoff year>" in them. Start with the official and primary sources the plan names, then widen. If what you find is mostly from before the cutoff, you're searching the wrong period: change your queries.
3. **Read the sources.** Open each one you rely on and find the passage that supports what you say. A search result's snippet is not enough.
4. **Check every date on the source itself.** Material published after the cutoff counts as new. You may use an older source for context, but label it as older.
5. **Compare like with like.** Before treating a figure as a change, check that it measures the same thing the same way as the report: the same scale or metric, the same version of a benchmark, survey or index, the same population and setup. When it doesn't, say so in the source's entry and in your summary, and don't count the gap as a change or a disagreement. A figure on a different measure shows a direction only with something that ties the two together (a series that overlaps both, a published calibration, independent evidence); name it, or say the direction is unknown. A lower score on a harder benchmark is not a decline.
6. **Follow the source guidance.** Prefer primary sources: the law over an article about the law, the statistics release over a news story about it. Use news for events and their dates. Don't rely on the kinds of sources the plan excludes.
7. **When a site refuses you,** look for the same document elsewhere before settling for coverage of it. Official documents are often mirrored or archived:
   - U.S. federal documents: govinfo.gov has public laws, the Federal Register and presidential documents; uscode.house.gov has the U.S. Code; everycrsreport.com has Congressional Research Service reports; agencies often repost their own documents on component or service sites.
   - Elsewhere: official journals and legislation portals, the issuing body's other sites, and web archives such as the Internet Archive.

   If you end up relying on coverage instead of the document, say so in the source's entry and lower your confidence if the point depends on it.
8. **Stop when the picture is clear,** or after a reasonable effort finds nothing. "Nothing new found" is a valid result. Report it with the searches you tried, so the user can judge how hard you looked.

## The verdict

For a fact, pick one:

- **Still holds:** recent sources show it is still true today. A fact you found no recent evidence about is "nothing new found", not "still holds".
- **Strengthened:** newer evidence supports it more firmly, and the evidence leans that way as a whole.
- **Weakened:** newer evidence casts doubt on it or narrows it, and the evidence leans that way as a whole.
- **Overturned:** it is no longer true.
- **Superseded:** newer figures or a newer version replace it, though the fact was right at the time.
- **Contested:** credible sources disagree about the same thing at the same time, or the newer evidence points both ways. Use it when the evidence splits, not when one weak source dissents, and not when sources measure different things (see step 5). Your summary gives each side and its strongest source, without picking a winner.
- **Nothing new found.**

Later evidence is not a disagreement. When a source after the cutoff reports that things have moved on (newer models score higher, a law was replaced), the fact is superseded, strengthened, weakened or overturned, even if the source says the report's statement no longer holds.

For a data source: **newer release** (give the old and new figures for what the report used), **discontinued or replaced** (name the replacement, if any) or **no newer release**.

For an angle: **significant developments**, **minor developments** or **nothing new found**. For an angle on other views of a claim: **contrary views found** (who argues otherwise, since when, and on what evidence), **agreeing views only** or **nothing new found**. A contrary view argues against the claim on its own terms; a source reporting that things have moved on since the report is a development, not a contrary view.

Then rate your confidence: **high** when primary sources settle it, **medium** when the evidence is good but indirect or partial, **low** when it rests on few or weak sources.

```markdown
# <item id>: <the item, short>

- **Verdict:** <verdict>
- **Confidence:** <high | medium | low>

## Summary

<Two to four sentences: what changed and when. For a data source, the old and new figures side by side, with the release dates.>

## Sources

1. <Full citation: author or issuer, title, publisher or outlet, date.> <URL or DOI>
   - **Type:** <legislation | government | intergovernmental | research | news | company | other>
   - **Published:** <date> <(before the cutoff: context only), if so>
   - **Excerpt:** "<one or two sentences, verbatim, that support your summary>"
   - **Bearing:** <how this source bears on the item>
   - **Measure:** <only when it measures differently from the report: another scale, version or setup, and what that means for comparing>

## Searches tried

- <query>
- …
```

Never include a source you didn't open, and never write an excerpt you didn't read in the source.
