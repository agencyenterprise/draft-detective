# Evaluation of the Pathways Reentry Employment Program: Findings from the First Three Years

## Chapter 1. Introduction

Each year, a large number of people are released from jails and prisons back into Harlow County, and a very large share of them return to neighborhoods where stable, well-paid work is difficult to find. Employment after release has long been viewed by researchers and practitioners as one of the most important and essential supports for a successful return to the community (Okafor, 2018; Lindqvist and Barrow, 2020). People who find work quickly after release are less likely to be rearrested, more likely to secure stable housing, and more likely to reconnect with family members. At the same time, it is important to note that people leaving incarceration face a really wide range of obstacles, including gaps in work history, criminal record screening by employers, limited access to transportation, and unmet health needs.

In response to these concerns, the Harlow County Board of Supervisors approved funding in the spring of 2022 for a new initiative known as the Pathways Reentry Employment Program, which is referred to throughout this report simply as Pathways. Pathways was launched by the Harlow County Office of Reentry Services in October 2022 and is operated in partnership with a training vendor, a case management provider, and a network of local employers. The program connects people returning from jail or prison with paid training, registered apprenticeships, job placement assistance, and ongoing case management. Funding for the first three years of operation was provided by the county in the amount of $4.2 million, drawn from the county general fund and from the Workforce Opportunity Levy that was approved by voters in 2021.

The pilot was designed to reduce the time between release and stable employment for participants in its first cohort. Pathways is located in three service hubs across the county, and each hub has a site coordinator and two employment specialists.

The three hubs are the Eastgate Hub in the city of Brennan Falls, the Riverside Hub in Millbrook, and the North Valley Hub in the town of Calder. The hubs were selected by county planners due to the fact that they are located in or near the communities to which the largest numbers of people return after release. Each hub serves a somewhat different population and labor market. Eastgate is the largest and most urban of the three, Riverside sits along a corridor of warehouses and light manufacturing, and North Valley serves a mostly rural area where public transit is very limited. These differences come up again and again throughout this report, because they shaped how the program was delivered and what participants were able to achieve.

### 1.1 Purpose of This Evaluation

In early 2025, our research team was engaged by the Office of Reentry Services in order to conduct an independent evaluation of the first three years of Pathways. The evaluation had two main parts. The first part was a process evaluation, which examined how the program was put into practice, whether services were delivered as intended, and what challenges were encountered along the way. The second part was an outcome evaluation, which examined employment, earnings, and justice system outcomes for people who enrolled in the program between October 2022 and June 2025. Both parts of the evaluation were intended to inform decisions that will be made by the county about whether to continue, expand, or modify the program at the end of its current funding period.

It is worth stating at the outset what this evaluation is able to tell us and what it is not able to tell us. Because Pathways was not implemented with random assignment, we are not able to make strong causal claims about the effect of the program on participant outcomes. We were, however, able to construct a comparison group from county administrative records, and we use that comparison group to provide some context for the outcomes that were observed among participants. The limitations of this approach are discussed in more detail in Chapter 3, and readers are encouraged to keep them in mind when they read the outcome findings that are presented in Chapter 6.

### 1.2 Overview of Key Findings

Over its first three years, Pathways has raised the profile of and normalized paid apprenticeships as the default route into employment for people leaving incarceration, and county agencies now refer to it in their own discharge planning. Barriers that can be addressed through better scheduling were common in the first year, but most hubs had resolved them by the end of the second.

A total of 612 people were enrolled in Pathways between October 2022 and June 2025, out of 1,034 people who were referred to the program during the same period. Of those who enrolled, 389 people, or 63.6 percent, had obtained employment by the end of June 2025. The median time from release to first employment among participants who found work was 74 days, compared with 118 days among a comparison group of people released during the same period who did not enroll. Retention was also fairly strong, with 71.0 percent of employed participants still working 90 days after they were hired. These findings are encouraging, but it is important to note that they varied quite a bit by hub and by the year in which participants enrolled.

The program also experienced a number of significant implementation challenges. The most serious of these was turnover in key leadership and partner roles during the first year, including the departure of the original program director and the original training vendor. Other challenges included delays in referral processing, transportation barriers in the North Valley service area, and inconsistent data entry practices across the three hubs. Many of these challenges had been addressed by the third year of the program, but some of them are still ongoing at this point in time and will need continued attention from program leadership.

### 1.3 Audience and Use of This Report

This report has been written for several audiences. The primary audience is the leadership of the Office of Reentry Services and the members of the Board of Supervisors who will make decisions about the future of the program. A second audience includes staff at the hubs and at partner agencies, who we hope will find the detailed findings useful in their day-to-day work. A third audience includes practitioners and funders in other jurisdictions who are considering similar programs and who may be able to learn from the experience of Pathways. We have tried to write the report in a way that is accessible to each and every one of these audiences, although some sections, particularly the methods chapter, are necessarily somewhat technical in nature.

We would like to stress that this evaluation could not have been completed without the very generous cooperation of program staff, partner agencies, employers, and, most importantly, participants. A large number of people gave their time to speak with us, and many of them shared personal experiences that were difficult to talk about. Their willingness to participate in interviews was greatly appreciated by the research team, and we have done our best to represent their views accurately and fairly. All names of participants that appear in this report are pseudonyms, and identifying details have been changed or removed in order to protect the confidentiality of those who were interviewed.

## Chapter 2. Program Background

### 2.1 Origins of the Program

The idea for Pathways was first raised in 2020 by a working group that had been convened by the Harlow County Office of Reentry Services in order to review the county's approach to supporting people after release. The working group included representatives from the county sheriff's office, the county probation department, the regional workforce board, several community-based organizations, and two people who had themselves been incarcerated. Over the course of roughly eighteen months, the working group reviewed local data, heard testimony from service providers, and studied programs that had been implemented in other counties. Its final report, which was issued in late 2021, concluded that the county's existing reentry services were fragmented and that very few of them were focused on employment in any sustained way.

One of the central observations made by the working group was that people leaving the county jail were typically released with little or no connection to employment services. Referrals to the regional workforce board were made on an informal basis by jail staff, and there was no system in place for tracking whether those referrals were ever followed up on. The working group also found that the training programs that did exist were mostly unpaid, which meant that people who needed income right away had a really strong incentive to skip training and take whatever short-term work they could find (Haverford and Lin, 2019). Based on these observations, the working group recommended that the county fund a new program that would combine paid training, apprenticeships, and case management under a single coordinated structure.

The recommendation was adopted by the Board of Supervisors in March 2022, and a request for proposals for a training vendor and a case management provider was issued by the county shortly afterward. Planning for the program took place over the summer of 2022, and the first participants were enrolled in October of that year. It is important to note that the planning period was quite short, and several of the staff members we interviewed felt that the program would have benefited from a longer period of time in which to develop procedures, hire staff, and build relationships with employers before the first participants arrived.

### 2.2 Program Model

The Pathways program model is built around four core components, which are intended to be delivered in a planned sequence. The first component is intake and assessment, which is conducted by an employment specialist at one of the three hubs, ideally within two weeks of a person's release. The second component is case management, which is provided by a case manager from New Horizons Family Services and which continues throughout a participant's time in the program. The third component is paid training, which is delivered by the program's training vendor and which typically lasts between six and twelve weeks. The fourth component is placement, which can take the form of either a registered apprenticeship with a partner employer or direct placement into a job.

Along with these four core components, participants are eligible to receive supportive services that are intended to remove practical barriers to employment. These include transit passes, gas cards, work clothing and tools, assistance with obtaining identification documents, and short-term help with rent in some cases. Supportive services are requested by the case manager, approved by staff at the Office of Reentry Services, and paid for out of a flexible fund that is administered by the county. The size of this fund was set at $310,000 per year for the first three years of the program. Table 1 summarizes the core components of the program, the staff who are responsible for each one, and the intended timing of each component.

Table 1. Core Components of the Pathways Program Model

| Component | Responsible Staff | Intended Timing |
|---|---|---|
| Intake and assessment | Hub employment specialist | Within 14 days of release |
| Case management | New Horizons case manager | Throughout enrollment (up to 18 months) |
| Paid training | Training vendor instructors | Weeks 2 through 14 after intake |
| Apprenticeship or job placement | Hub employment specialist and employer partner | Following completion of training |
| Supportive services | Case manager, with ORS approval | As needed throughout enrollment |

Participants are paid an hourly stipend while they are in training, which was set at $15.50 per hour at the start of the program and was raised to $16.75 per hour in July 2024. Once participants enter an apprenticeship, their wages are paid by the employer, although a portion of those wages is reimbursed by the program during the first six months. Wage reimbursement was intended to encourage employers to take a chance on hiring people with criminal records, and many of the employers that we spoke with said that it played a very important role in their decision to participate. Participants who are placed directly into jobs rather than into apprenticeships do not receive a stipend after placement, but they do continue to receive case management and follow-up support from hub staff for up to twelve months.

### 2.3 Eligibility and Referral

To be eligible for Pathways, a person must be 18 years of age or older, must be a resident of Harlow County, and must have been released from a jail or prison within the previous 90 days. There are no restrictions based on the type of offense, although participants who are subject to certain conditions of supervision may be limited in the types of apprenticeships that are available to them. Referrals can be made by jail staff, by probation officers, by state parole agents, by community organizations, or by people themselves.

When a referral is received, it is first entered into the Pathways Case Tracking System by staff at the Office of Reentry Services, and it is then assigned to the hub that is closest to the person's address. The hub's site coordinator is responsible for contacting the person and scheduling an intake appointment. The program's written procedures call for first contact to be made within three business days of the referral being received, but as we discuss in Chapter 5, this timeline was frequently not met during the early part of the program due to the fact that referrals were processed in batches rather than as they arrived.

### 2.4 Partner Organizations

Pathways is administered by the Harlow County Office of Reentry Services, which employs the program director, a data manager, a contracts manager, and the site coordinators and employment specialists who work at the three hubs. Case management is provided under contract by New Horizons Family Services, a nonprofit organization that has provided social services in the county for more than thirty years. New Horizons employs seven case managers who are assigned to Pathways, with three based at Eastgate, two at Riverside, and two at North Valley.

The original training vendor for the program was the Keystone Skills Collaborative, a regional nonprofit that had previously provided pre-apprenticeship training in the building trades. Keystone delivered training to participants during the first year of the program, but its contract was ended by mutual agreement in September 2023, for reasons that we discuss in Chapter 5. After a gap of approximately three months, during which a limited amount of training was provided on an interim basis by Harlow Valley Community College, a new contract was awarded to the Tri-County Trades Institute, which began delivering training in January 2024. Tri-County offers training tracks in construction, warehousing and logistics, food production, and facilities maintenance.

The program also depends on a network of employer partners who agree to accept apprentices or to interview Pathways graduates for open positions. At the start of the program, nine employers had signed letters of commitment with the Office of Reentry Services. By June 2025, the number of active employer partners had grown to 23. The largest employer partners by number of placements are Ridgeline Logistics, Castellan Manufacturing, Brightwater Foods, Hollins Construction Group, and the facilities division of the Meridian Health System. A smaller number of placements have been made with restaurants, auto repair shops, and landscaping companies, mostly in the North Valley area where large employers are scarce.

### 2.5 Program Leadership and Staffing

The program was led during its first nine months by its founding director, who had been closely involved in the work of the original county working group. The founding director left the Office of Reentry Services in June 2023 in order to take a position in another state. The program was then managed on an interim basis by the deputy director of the Office of Reentry Services, who also had a number of other responsibilities at the time. A permanent director was hired in February 2024 and has remained in that role through the end of the evaluation period.

Staffing at the hubs has been relatively stable compared with program leadership, although there was some turnover among employment specialists during the first two years. Of the six employment specialist positions that were filled at the start of the program, four were still held by the same people in June 2025. Two of the three site coordinators had also been in their positions since the program began. Several of the hub staff members that we interviewed had personal experience with the justice system, and a number of participants told us that this made a very real difference in how comfortable they felt when they first came to the program. We return to the role of staff with lived experience in Chapter 7.

### 2.6 Funding and Budget

Total funding for the first three years of Pathways was $4.2 million, which was allocated by the Board of Supervisors in three annual installments of $1.4 million. Of this amount, approximately 38 percent was spent on hub staff and program administration, 27 percent was spent on the training vendor contracts, 15 percent was spent on the case management contract, 13 percent was spent on participant stipends and wage reimbursement, and the remaining 7 percent was spent on supportive services and other costs. Because training was interrupted for part of the first year, a portion of the training budget went unspent in that year and was carried over by the county into the second year.

At this point in time, funding for the program has been authorized through September 2026, and a decision about whether to renew the program for a further three years is expected to be made by the Board of Supervisors in the spring of 2026. Several board members have asked for evidence of the program's effectiveness to inform that decision, which is one of the main reasons why this evaluation was commissioned. It is our hope that the findings presented in the chapters that follow will be helpful to the board as it considers the future of the program.

## Chapter 3. Methods

### 3.1 Overview of the Evaluation Design

The evaluation used a mixed-methods design that combined the analysis of administrative data with qualitative interviews and a review of program documents. The design was developed by the research team in consultation with the Office of Reentry Services during the first two months of the project, and it was reviewed and approved by the county's research review committee in February 2025. In developing the design, we tried to strike a balance between what would be most useful to program leadership and what would be feasible given the time and resources that were available. The evaluation was conducted over a period of roughly nine months, from January through September 2025.

### 3.2 Process Evaluation

The process evaluation assessed whether Pathways delivered its core services as intended and whether participants received them in the planned sequence. The process evaluation research questions summarized in Table 2. We drew on enrollment records, program documents, and interviews with staff and participants.

Table 2. Process Evaluation Research Questions

| Number | Research Question |
|---|---|
| P1 | How many people were referred to and enrolled in Pathways, and how did they compare with the broader population of people released in the county? |
| P2 | To what extent did participants receive each of the core program components, and in what order? |
| P3 | How long did it take for participants to move from referral to intake, from intake to training, and from training to placement? |
| P4 | How did service delivery differ across the three hubs? |
| P5 | What factors supported the implementation of the program, and what challenges were encountered? |
| P6 | How did participants and staff perceive the program and its services? |

The first four research questions were answered mainly through the analysis of enrollment records that were extracted from the Pathways Case Tracking System. The last two research questions were answered mainly through interviews, although we also made use of program documents such as meeting minutes, quarterly reports to the Board of Supervisors, and partner contracts. It is important to note that some of the research questions could be answered more completely than others, due to the fact that certain types of information were not recorded consistently in the case tracking system during the first year of the program.

In staff interviews, we explored the factors that both supported the rollout of Pathways, as well as the barriers encountered during program rollout. Staff reported that the interview topics matched their own priorities for the program. We interviewed 24 staff members and 31 participants between March and August 2025.

The 24 staff members who were interviewed included 9 hub staff (all three site coordinators and all six employment specialists), 7 case managers from New Horizons Family Services, 5 administrators and managers from the Office of Reentry Services, and 3 instructors from the Tri-County Trades Institute. We also attempted to interview former staff members of the Keystone Skills Collaborative, but we were only able to reach one of them, and that person declined to be interviewed. Along with the staff interviews, we conducted 11 shorter interviews with representatives of employer partners, which were focused on their experiences hiring and supervising Pathways participants.

Participants were recruited for interviews by hub staff, who were asked to invite a mix of participants who were currently enrolled, participants who had completed the program, and participants who had left the program early. Of the 31 participants who were interviewed, 13 were from the Eastgate Hub, 10 were from the Riverside Hub, and 8 were from the North Valley Hub. Participants were offered a $40 gift card as a thank-you for their time. Interviews with participants lasted between 30 and 75 minutes and were conducted either in person at the hubs or by telephone, depending on the preference of the participant.

All interviews were conducted using semi-structured interview guides that had been developed by the research team. With the permission of interviewees, the interviews were audio recorded and transcribed. Transcripts were then coded by two members of the research team using a codebook that was developed through a combination of deductive codes drawn from the research questions and inductive codes that were identified during an initial reading of the transcripts. Disagreements in coding were discussed and resolved by the two coders, and the codebook was revised a total of three times over the course of the analysis.

We recognize that the participants who agreed to be interviewed may not be fully representative of all participants in the program. In particular, it is likely that participants who were more engaged with the program were more willing to be interviewed and more easily reached by hub staff. We made a very deliberate effort to include participants who had left the program early, and 7 of the 31 participants we interviewed fell into that category, but their views may still be underrepresented in our findings. We also recognize that hub staff had the ability to influence which participants were invited, and we tried to reduce this risk by asking for a list of all participants who had been contacted, not only those who had agreed.

### 3.3 Outcome Evaluation

The outcome evaluation examined employment, earnings, job retention, and justice system involvement for people who enrolled in Pathways. The outcome research questions are listed in Table 3. Data for the outcome evaluation were drawn from three main sources: the Pathways Case Tracking System, quarterly wage records that were provided by the state employment department under a data sharing agreement, and booking and release records that were provided by the Harlow County Sheriff's Office.

Table 3. Outcome Evaluation Research Questions

| Number | Research Question |
|---|---|
| O1 | What share of participants obtained employment, and how long did it take them to do so after release? |
| O2 | What were the wages and quarterly earnings of employed participants? |
| O3 | What share of employed participants remained employed 90 days and 180 days after hire? |
| O4 | What share of participants completed an apprenticeship? |
| O5 | What share of participants were reincarcerated within 12 months of release? |
| O6 | How did these outcomes compare with those of similar people who were released during the same period and did not enroll in Pathways? |

In order to address the sixth research question, we constructed a comparison group of people who had been released from the Harlow County jail between October 2022 and June 2025, who met the eligibility criteria for the program, and who did not enroll. The comparison group was matched to participants using propensity score methods on the basis of age, sex, race and ethnicity, length of the most recent period of incarceration, number of prior bookings, the most serious charge at the most recent booking, and the hub service area in which the person lived at the time of release. The final matched comparison group included 1,148 people.

It is very important to note that the comparison group differs from the participant group in ways that we were not able to measure. Most notably, people who chose to enroll in Pathways may have been more motivated to find work than people who did not enroll, and this difference could account for some or all of the differences in outcomes that we observed. For this reason, the comparisons that are presented in Chapter 6 should be understood as descriptive rather than causal. We believe that they provide useful context, but they should not be interpreted as estimates of the effect of the program.

### 3.4 Data Quality and Limitations

The quality of the data in the Pathways Case Tracking System improved a great deal over the course of the program. During the first year, a large number of records were missing key fields, such as the date of intake, the date of training enrollment, and the reason for exit. Data entry was performed by hub staff, and practices differed from one hub to another. In some cases, dates were entered weeks or months after the event in question, and in other cases they were not entered at all. Beginning in the spring of 2024, a new data manager was hired by the Office of Reentry Services, and a set of data entry standards was introduced across all three hubs. As a result, the completeness of records improved substantially in the second and third years.

In order to deal with missing data in the first year, we took several steps. Where possible, missing dates were filled in using information from other sources, such as training vendor attendance logs and case manager notes. Where this was not possible, we excluded records from specific analyses and reported the number of records that were excluded. Overall, between 4 and 11 percent of first-year records were excluded from individual analyses, depending on the variable in question, while fewer than 2 percent of records from later years were excluded from any analysis.

The state wage records that were used to measure employment and earnings also have certain limitations. These records only include employment that is covered by the state unemployment insurance system, which means that they do not capture self-employment, informal work, or work that is performed in other states. Because a number of participants reported doing informal or cash work, particularly in the North Valley area, it is likely that our wage record measures somewhat understate the true level of employment among participants. To address this limitation, we supplemented the wage records with placement information that was recorded by hub staff, and we report both measures where it is appropriate to do so.

Finally, the follow-up period that was available for the outcome evaluation was relatively short for participants who enrolled in the third year of the program. Participants who enrolled in early 2025 had only a few months of follow-up by the time the data were extracted, which limits what we can say about their longer-term outcomes. For the analysis of reincarceration, we restricted the sample to participants who enrolled during the first year of the program, for whom a full 12 months of follow-up was available. Readers should keep these limitations in mind when they interpret the findings presented later in this report.

## Chapter 4. Program Participants and Services

### 4.1 Referrals and Enrollment

Between October 2022 and June 2025, a total of 1,034 people were referred to Pathways. Of these, 612 people completed an intake appointment and were formally enrolled in the program, which represents an overall enrollment rate of 59.2 percent. The number of referrals grew steadily over the life of the program, from 291 in the first year to 402 in the second year and 341 in the first nine months of the third year. The growth in referrals was attributed by program staff to a number of factors, including stronger relationships with jail discharge planners, the inclusion of Pathways in standard probation orientation materials, and word of mouth among people who had already participated in the program.

Enrollment rates differed somewhat across the three hubs. The Eastgate Hub received 402 referrals and enrolled 248 people, for an enrollment rate of 61.7 percent. The Riverside Hub received 351 referrals and enrolled 207 people, for an enrollment rate of 59.0 percent. The North Valley Hub received 281 referrals and enrolled 157 people, for an enrollment rate of 55.9 percent. Staff at North Valley told us that the lower enrollment rate at their hub was largely due to the fact that many referred individuals lived a considerable distance from the hub and had no reliable way of getting there for an intake appointment.

The reasons why referred individuals did not enroll were recorded in the case tracking system for about two-thirds of the 422 people who were referred but did not enroll. Among those with a recorded reason, the most common reasons were that the person could not be reached after the referral was received (41 percent), that the person had already found a job on their own (19 percent), that the person declined to participate (17 percent), and that the person had been rearrested or had moved out of the county before intake could take place (14 percent). The remaining 9 percent of cases were recorded as "other." It is important to note that a very large share of people who could not be reached were referred during the first year of the program, when referrals were often processed by the county several weeks after they were received.

Enrollment by program year followed a similar pattern to referrals. A total of 168 people enrolled during the first program year (October 2022 through September 2023), 241 people enrolled during the second program year (October 2023 through September 2024), and 203 people enrolled during the first nine months of the third program year (October 2024 through June 2025). The relatively low number of enrollments in the first year reflects both the time that was needed to build up referral relationships and the disruption that was caused by the change in training vendor in the fall of 2023.

About three-quarters of participants (77.0 percent) started paid training, and 62.7 percent of all participants completed training. Put another way, 81.5 percent of those who started training went on to complete it, which program staff regarded as a very positive result.

A total of 214 participants, or 35.0 percent, started a registered apprenticeship with one of the program's employer partners. Apprenticeships were most common at Riverside, where 39.6 percent of participants started one, and least common at North Valley, where only 24.2 percent did so. The lower rate at North Valley was explained by staff as being due to the fact that there are very few employers in the area that are large enough to sponsor registered apprentices, and that participants who lived in the area often could not commute to employers located elsewhere in the county. Participants who did not enter apprenticeships were generally offered job placement services instead, which included help with resumes, interview preparation, and direct referrals to employers with open positions.

Supportive services were received by 65.7 percent of participants overall. Rates of supportive service receipt were highest at North Valley (73.2 percent), which mainly reflected the large number of gas cards and vehicle repair payments that were authorized for participants at that hub. Transit passes were the most common type of supportive service at Eastgate and Riverside, followed by work clothing and tools. The average amount of supportive services received per participant who received any supportive services was $612, although this amount varied a great deal, from less than $50 for some participants to more than $3,000 for a small number of participants who received rental assistance.

### 4.4 Timing and Sequence of Services

One of the main goals of the program was to connect participants to services quickly after their release. The program's written procedures call for intake to take place within 14 days of release, for training to begin within two weeks of intake, and for placement to follow promptly after the completion of training. In practice, these timelines were met for some participants but not for others, and there was a great deal of improvement in timeliness over the life of the program.

The median number of days from referral to intake fell from 19 days in the first program year to 9 days in the second program year and 6 days in the third. This improvement was attributed by staff mainly to changes that were made by the Office of Reentry Services in the way that referrals were processed, which are described in more detail in Chapter 5. The median number of days from intake to the start of training was 16 days overall, but this figure was much higher for participants who enrolled between October and December 2023, when no permanent training vendor was in place. For those participants, the median wait for training was 58 days, and a number of them left the program before training became available.

We also examined whether participants received services in the planned sequence, which is intake, followed by case management and training, followed by placement. Overall, 68 percent of participants who received placement services had completed training before being placed, which is consistent with the program model. The remaining 32 percent were placed into jobs without having completed training, either because they had not started training at all or because they left training early in order to take a job. Staff told us that it is not always in a participant's best interest to complete training if a good job offer becomes available, and we agree that a certain amount of flexibility in the sequence of services is entirely appropriate. At the same time, the large share of participants who skipped training in the first year suggests that some of these departures from the planned sequence were driven by delays in the program rather than by the needs of participants.

### 4.5 Participant Perspectives on Services

The participants we interviewed were, on the whole, very positive about the services they received. Many of them spoke warmly about their case managers and employment specialists, and several said that Pathways was the first program they had been involved with where staff seemed to really care about what happened to them. One participant at Riverside told us that his employment specialist "checked on me every week, even after I got the job, and that kept me going when I wanted to quit." Another participant at Eastgate said that she would not have been able to stay in training without the stipend, due to the fact that she was supporting two children and had no other source of income at the time.

Participants were more mixed in their views of the training itself. Participants who had been trained by the Tri-County Trades Institute were generally satisfied with the quality of the instruction and felt that the skills they learned were relevant to the jobs that were available. Participants who had been trained during the first year, when the Keystone Skills Collaborative was the vendor, were more critical, and several of them said that the training felt disorganized and was poorly matched to the jobs that they were eventually offered. A small number of participants at North Valley said that they had been placed in a training track that did not interest them, simply because it was the only track that was available at a time and place that they could get to.

Participants also raised concerns about the length of time it sometimes took to receive supportive services. A number of participants said that they had waited two or three weeks for a gas card or a transit pass, during which time they had difficulty getting to training or to work. Requests for supportive services were approved by the Office of Reentry Services rather than by case managers directly, and staff confirmed that the approval process was often slow, particularly during periods when the program was short-staffed at the county level. We return to this issue in the recommendations presented in Chapter 7.

## Chapter 5. Implementation Challenges

### 5.1 Overview

Like most new programs, Pathways encountered a number of challenges during its first three years of operation. Some of these challenges were anticipated by program planners, while others were not. In this chapter, we describe the most significant challenges that were identified through our interviews with staff, partners, and participants, and through our review of program documents. For each challenge, we describe how it affected the delivery of services, how program staff and partners responded to it, and whether it had been resolved by the end of the evaluation period. It is important to note that many of the challenges described here are common to reentry employment programs more generally, and they should not be taken as evidence that Pathways was poorly managed (Sandoval and Achebe, 2022).

### 5.2 Delays in Referral Processing

The most frequently mentioned operational challenge during the first year of the program was the length of time it took for referrals to reach the hubs. During the first year, referrals from the jail and from probation were sent to the Office of Reentry Services by email or fax and were entered into the case tracking system by a single administrative assistant, who also had a large number of other duties. Referrals were typically processed once or twice a week rather than on the day they were received. As a result, by the time a hub received a referral, the person in question had often been out of custody for a week or more, and in a number of cases, the contact information that had been provided at the time of release was no longer accurate.

Hub staff told us that these delays made their jobs very much harder. Several employment specialists described spending a great deal of time trying to track down people who had been referred weeks earlier, often without success. One site coordinator said that during the first year, "we were basically chasing ghosts, because by the time we got the name, the person had already moved on." As noted in Chapter 4, the inability to reach referred individuals was the single most common reason for non-enrollment, and the large majority of those cases occurred in the first year of the program.

In the spring of 2023, the Office of Reentry Services began working with the sheriff's office on a direct referral process, under which jail discharge planners would enter referrals into the case tracking system themselves. This process was fully implemented by September 2023, and it was later extended to county probation officers in early 2024. Following these changes, the median time from referral to intake fell substantially, as shown in Chapter 4. Staff were in general agreement that the direct referral process was one of the most important improvements that had been made to the program, and several of them said that it should have been put in place from the very beginning.

### 5.3 Scheduling Conflicts

A second operational challenge that came up again and again in our interviews was the difficulty participants faced in fitting program activities around their other obligations. A large number of participants were under some form of community supervision, which required them to attend regular meetings with probation or parole officers, and some of them were also required to attend substance use treatment, drug testing, or court appearances. During the first year, training sessions were scheduled by the training vendor without taking these obligations into account, and participants were frequently forced to choose between attending training and meeting the conditions of their supervision.

Program staff responded to this problem in several ways. Beginning in late 2023, employment specialists started to collect information about participants' supervision schedules at intake, and they shared this information with the training vendor and with case managers. The Office of Reentry Services also reached an agreement with the county probation department under which probation officers would, wherever possible, schedule check-ins outside of training hours for Pathways participants. The Tri-County Trades Institute, for its part, agreed to offer training sessions in both morning and afternoon blocks at its two largest sites. By the end of the second program year, staff at Eastgate and Riverside reported that scheduling conflicts had become relatively rare, although staff at North Valley said that they were still a concern for participants with treatment obligations in other parts of the county.

### 5.4 Transportation Barriers

Transportation was a persistent barrier for participants at all three hubs, but it was by far the most serious at North Valley. As noted in Chapter 4, more than six in ten participants at North Valley did not have a valid driver's license, and the area is served by only one bus route, which runs on a limited schedule and does not operate on Sundays. Participants who lived in outlying parts of the service area told us that it could take them two hours or more to reach the hub by bus, and some of them said that they had simply given up on the program because they had no reliable way of getting to it.

North Valley staff made a number of efforts in order to address this challenge. They arranged for some training sessions to be held at a community center in Calder, they used supportive services funds to pay for vehicle repairs and gas cards for participants who had access to a car, and in 2024 they began operating a van on a limited basis to transport participants to training and job interviews. The van was purchased with a one-time grant from a local foundation, and it was driven by a part-time driver whose position was funded through the program's supportive services budget. Staff felt that the van had made a very real difference, but they also noted that it was only able to serve a small number of participants at any one time and that it had no dedicated source of long-term funding.

Transportation also affected the types of jobs and apprenticeships that participants were able to accept. Several employer partners, including Ridgeline Logistics and Castellan Manufacturing, operate shifts that begin very early in the morning or end late at night, when public transit is not available. A number of participants at all three hubs told us that they had turned down job offers from these employers due to the fact that they could not get to work at the required times. Employment specialists said that they now take shift times and transit access into account when making referrals to employers, but that this sometimes limits the options that are available to participants.

### 5.5 Data Systems and Reporting

The fourth operational challenge was related to data systems. The Pathways Case Tracking System was built by a county contractor on a very short timeline before the program launched, and hub staff told us that it was difficult to use and did not match the way that they actually worked. Fields for recording key events, such as the start and end of training, were added to the system several months after the program had begun, and there were no written guidelines for staff on how and when to enter data. As we described in Chapter 3, this led to a large number of incomplete records during the first year.

The data problems also made it difficult for program leadership to monitor how the program was performing. Quarterly reports to the Board of Supervisors during the first year were prepared by hand by the deputy director, using a combination of data from the case tracking system and information that was collected directly from the hubs by email. Several administrators told us that they had very little confidence in the accuracy of the numbers in those early reports. Following the hiring of a dedicated data manager in the spring of 2024, data entry standards were introduced, staff received training on how to use the system, and a monthly data review meeting was set up between the data manager and each of the site coordinators. Staff felt that these changes had greatly improved the quality of the data, and our own review of the records supports this view.

### 5.6 Leadership and Partner Turnover

The structural challenges faced by the program were, in many respects, more serious and harder to address than the operational challenges discussed above. Chief among them was turnover in leadership and among key partners during the first year. As described in Chapter 2, the founding director left the program in June 2023, after only nine months, and the program was then managed on an interim basis for eight months by the deputy director of the Office of Reentry Services. Over roughly the same period, the relationship between the Office of Reentry Services and the original training vendor deteriorated, and the vendor's contract was ended in September 2023.

The reasons for the end of the Keystone contract were described to us in somewhat different terms by different interviewees. County administrators told us that Keystone had struggled to recruit enough instructors, that training sessions were frequently cancelled at short notice, and that the vendor had been slow to adapt its curriculum to the needs of the participant population. What is clear, however, is that the end of the contract left the program without a permanent training provider for roughly three months, during which a number of participants left the program.

In the case of Pathways, staff turnover was a persistent challenge from the outset: the departure of the program director in the first year left a gap in leadership, which was further compounded by the departure of the original training vendor. In addition to losing some institutional knowledge, the program lost two established referral relationships with local employers. Future programs of this kind would, therefore, benefit from building stronger succession plans at the outset, such as cross-training deputy staff and documenting partner agreements in writing.

The two employers in question, Danforth Metalworks and Selby Packaging, had both worked closely with Keystone for several years before Pathways was launched, and their relationships with the program had been managed mostly through personal contacts between Keystone staff and their hiring managers. When Keystone left the program, these relationships were not transferred to the Office of Reentry Services or to the new training vendor, and neither employer accepted a Pathways participant after the fall of 2023. Hub staff told us that they had made several attempts to reestablish these relationships, but that by the time they did so, both employers had begun working with other workforce programs in the region.

### 5.7 Coordination Among Partner Organizations

Pathways depended on working well as a multi-partner system, and leadership turnover can test that network's cohesion and strength. As the original program designers were no longer present to articulate its vision or take responsibility for its design gaps and inconsistencies, the tensions between partner agencies can become particularly salient. Participants may not notice these tensions directly, but they appear to affect how quickly referrals move between agencies.

In our interviews, we heard about several areas where the expectations of different partner organizations were not well aligned. One of these areas concerned the division of responsibilities between hub employment specialists and New Horizons case managers. The program's written procedures did not clearly specify which of these staff members was responsible for following up with participants who stopped attending training, and as a result, this follow-up was sometimes done by both and sometimes by neither. Several case managers told us that they felt that they were being asked to take on responsibilities that were not included in their contract, while some employment specialists felt that case managers were not doing enough to keep participants engaged in the program.

A second area of disagreement concerned the approval of supportive services. As described in Chapter 4, requests for supportive services were made by case managers but approved by the Office of Reentry Services, and the approval process was often slow. Case managers told us that they did not understand why they were not able to approve small requests, such as a transit pass, themselves, and that the delays undermined their credibility with participants. County administrators, for their part, said that the approval process had been put in place in order to ensure that public funds were spent appropriately and that it would be difficult to change it without the approval of the county's finance office.

By the third year of the program, many of these tensions had eased somewhat. The new program director, who started in February 2024, established a monthly partners' meeting that brings together leadership from the Office of Reentry Services, New Horizons, Tri-County, and representatives of employer partners. Several interviewees told us that these meetings had improved communication a great deal and had helped to resolve a number of long-standing disagreements. A revised memorandum of understanding that sets out the roles and responsibilities of each partner was also being drafted at the time of our interviews, although it had not yet been finalized by the end of the evaluation period. It remains to be seen whether these changes will be enough to sustain good working relationships among partners over the longer term, particularly if there is further turnover in key positions.

## Chapter 6. Outcomes

### 6.1 Overview

In this chapter, we present findings on the outcomes of people who enrolled in Pathways between October 2022 and June 2025. We focus on five main outcomes: whether participants obtained employment, how long it took them to do so, how much they earned, whether they remained employed, and whether they were reincarcerated. Where it is possible to do so, we compare the outcomes of participants with those of the matched comparison group that was described in Chapter 3. As we noted in that chapter, these comparisons are descriptive in nature and should not be read as estimates of the causal effect of the program.

We also present findings separately by hub and by the program year in which participants enrolled. These breakdowns are important due to the fact that the program changed in a number of significant ways over its first three years, and outcomes for participants who enrolled during the first year, when the program was experiencing the most serious implementation challenges, may not be representative of the outcomes that can be expected from the program as it currently operates.

### 6.2 Employment

By the end of June 2025, a total of 389 participants, or 63.6 percent of all participants, had obtained employment, as measured by either state wage records or placement records maintained by hub staff. Of these 389 participants, 214 had started a registered apprenticeship, and 175 had been placed directly into a job or had found a job on their own while enrolled in the program. Among the matched comparison group, 41.2 percent of people had obtained employment covered by the state wage records during the same follow-up period. Because the comparison group measure is based only on wage records, we also calculated the employment rate for participants using wage records alone, which was 58.7 percent. Even on this more conservative basis, the employment rate among participants was considerably higher than among the comparison group.

Table 6 presents the main employment outcomes for participants by hub. Employment rates were very similar at Eastgate (66.9 percent) and Riverside (67.6 percent), but were substantially lower at North Valley (52.9 percent). The median number of days from release to first employment also varied across hubs, from 66 days at Riverside and 69 days at Eastgate to 97 days at North Valley. These differences are consistent with what we heard from staff and participants about the transportation barriers and the limited number of employers in the North Valley service area, as discussed in Chapter 5.

Even so, the difference of more than six weeks between the two groups is large enough that it is very unlikely to be explained by this measurement issue alone.

We are not able to say with certainty which of these changes, if any, was responsible for the improvement, and it is possible that other factors, such as changes in the local labor market, also played a role. Regional unemployment rates were relatively stable over the period in question, however, which suggests that the labor market alone is unlikely to account for the full extent of the improvement that was observed.

### 6.4 Earnings and Wages

The median starting hourly wage among employed participants was $19.40. Participants who entered registered apprenticeships earned a median starting wage of $21.10 per hour, while participants who were placed directly into jobs earned a median starting wage of $18.25 per hour. Wages were lowest at North Valley, where the median starting wage was $17.90, reflecting both the types of jobs that were available in the area and the smaller share of participants who entered apprenticeships. For context, the county's minimum wage was $15.00 per hour throughout the evaluation period.

Using state wage records, we also examined quarterly earnings in the first full calendar quarter after participants were hired. Median earnings in that quarter were $6,870 among participants, compared with $4,920 among employed members of the comparison group. This difference reflects a combination of higher hourly wages and more hours worked among participants. A large number of comparison group members appear to have worked in temporary or part-time jobs, which is consistent with what the research literature tells us about the types of work that people typically find after release when they are not connected to employment services (Petrakis and Dunmore, 2017).

### 6.5 Job Retention

Retention in employment is a very important outcome for reentry employment programs, because short periods of employment followed by long periods of joblessness are common among people with criminal records. Of the 389 participants who obtained employment, 276, or 71.0 percent, were still employed 90 days after they were hired. We were also able to measure retention at 180 days for the 301 participants who had been hired by the end of December 2024. Among this group, 193 participants, or 64.1 percent, were still employed 180 days after they were hired. Retention at 90 days was highest at Riverside (72.9 percent) and Eastgate (72.3 percent) and lowest at North Valley (65.1 percent).

Retention was considerably higher among participants who entered apprenticeships than among those who were placed directly into jobs. Of the 214 participants who started an apprenticeship, 97 had completed it by June 2025, 61 were still actively enrolled, and 56 had left before completing it. This means that 45.3 percent of apprentices had completed their apprenticeship and a further 28.5 percent were still active, while 26.2 percent had left early. Employer partners told us that the most common reasons for early exits were attendance problems, conflicts with supervisors, and, in a smaller number of cases, rearrest. Several employers also said that early exits were much less common among apprentices who had a strong relationship with their case manager or employment specialist.

It is worth noting that employers were, on the whole, very satisfied with the participants they had hired through the program. Of the 11 employer representatives that we interviewed, 9 said that they would continue to hire Pathways participants after the end of the wage reimbursement period, and several said that Pathways apprentices had performed as well as or better than other entry-level employees. Two employers expressed concerns about the level of support that was available to apprentices after hours, and one said that communication with the program had been inconsistent during the period when the program was without a permanent director.

### 6.6 Reincarceration

The final outcome that we examined was reincarceration, which we defined as a new booking into the county jail or a new admission to a state prison within 12 months of release. Because a full 12 months of follow-up was only available for participants who enrolled during the first program year, our analysis of this outcome was restricted to those 168 participants. Among them, 21 participants, or 12.5 percent, were reincarcerated within 12 months of release. Among members of the comparison group who were released during the same period, the rate of reincarceration within 12 months was 19.8 percent.

This difference is encouraging, but it should be interpreted with a great deal of caution. The number of participants in the first-year cohort is relatively small, and as we noted in Chapter 3, there may be important differences between participants and comparison group members that we were not able to measure. It is also worth noting that the first-year cohort enrolled during the period when the program was experiencing its most serious implementation challenges, and so it is possible that reincarceration rates for later cohorts will be lower still. We recommend that the county continue to track this outcome for later cohorts as more follow-up time becomes available.

### 6.7 Summary of Outcome Findings

Taken together, the outcome findings suggest that Pathways participants obtained employment more often and more quickly than similar people who did not enroll in the program, that they earned higher wages, and that they were less likely to be reincarcerated within a year of release. These findings were strongest at Eastgate and Riverside and weakest at North Valley, and they improved a great deal between the first and second years of the program. While we cannot rule out the possibility that some of these differences are due to differences in the motivation or circumstances of participants, the overall pattern of results is consistent with the conclusion that the program is having a positive effect on the employment prospects of people leaving incarceration in Harlow County.

## Chapter 7. Recommendations and Conclusions

### 7.1 Lessons Learned

Several important lessons emerged from the analysis of enrollment records, conversations with Pathways partner agencies and participants, and the review of relevant literature. We present them in order of the number of hubs they affect.

The first and most fundamental lesson is that speed matters. The improvement in outcomes between the first and second years coincided with changes that shortened the time between release and intake, and participants who were contacted quickly were far more likely to enroll than those who were contacted weeks later. The second lesson is that the program depends very heavily on a small number of key people and relationships, and that it is vulnerable when those people leave. The third lesson is that a single program model cannot be applied in the same way across very different local settings, as the experience of North Valley makes clear.

### 7.2 Recommendations Affecting All Three Hubs

Our first recommendation is that the Office of Reentry Services should give case managers the authority to approve small supportive service requests directly, up to a set dollar limit. At present, requests are reviewed by county staff before they are approved, and the delays that result from this process were described by participants and case managers alike as a real obstacle to staying in training and keeping a job. A limit of $150 per request would cover the large majority of transit passes, gas cards, and work clothing purchases, while larger requests could continue to be reviewed by the county.

Our second recommendation is that the program should finalize and adopt the revised memorandum of understanding among partners as soon as possible. The memorandum should clearly set out which staff are responsible for following up with participants who stop attending training, how information is to be shared between hub staff and case managers, and how employer relationships are to be documented and maintained.

Our third recommendation is that the Office of Reentry Services should develop a written succession plan for the program director role and for the management of the training vendor contract. As we described in Chapter 5, the loss of the founding director and the original vendor in the same year set the program back considerably, and the program lost two employer partners because their relationships were held by individuals rather than by the program. Employer contacts, partner agreements, and key procedures should be recorded in a shared location that is accessible to more than one person at each partner organization.

Our fourth recommendation is that the county should continue to invest in the data system and in the data manager position. The improvements that were made in 2024 have made it possible, for the first time, to track the timing and sequence of services with reasonable accuracy. We recommend that the program build on this foundation by adding regular dashboards for site coordinators and by continuing to obtain state wage records on a quarterly basis, so that employment and retention can be monitored on an ongoing basis rather than only during periodic evaluations.

### 7.3 Recommendations Affecting Two Hubs

Our fifth recommendation concerns the Eastgate and Riverside hubs, where the largest numbers of apprentices are placed. At both hubs, employers raised concerns about the level of support that was available to apprentices outside of normal business hours, particularly for those working early-morning or overnight shifts. We recommend that the program explore the possibility of offering evening check-in hours at these two hubs, or of assigning an on-call employment specialist who can be reached by apprentices and employers when problems arise outside of the regular schedule.

Indeed, research on reentry employment programs suggests that trust between staff and participants shapes engagement; in particular, people returning from incarceration emphasized the value of staff with lived experience of the justice system (Moreno and Clarke, 2021). For a program like Pathways, it may be particularly helpful to conduct early assessments to explore factors like prevalence of unstable housing, transportation barriers and childcare needs and also better understand how potential participants perceive the program. One related recommendation for funders is to dedicate resources up front to hiring outreach staff, ideally those closely familiar with the local realities of the participant population.

### 7.4 Recommendations Affecting One Hub

Our sixth and final recommendation concerns North Valley, which had the lowest rates of enrollment, training completion, apprenticeship, and employment of the three hubs. We recommend that the county identify a long-term source of funding for the North Valley transportation van and consider expanding the service to a second vehicle. We also recommend that the program work with the Tri-County Trades Institute to offer at least one full training track at the community center in Calder, so that participants who live in the area do not have to travel long distances in order to attend training. Finally, we recommend that hub staff continue their efforts to recruit smaller employers in the North Valley area as partners, even if these employers are not able to sponsor registered apprenticeships.

### 7.5 Conclusions

Pathways has now been in operation for three years, and during that time it has enrolled 612 people, helped 389 of them find work, and placed 214 of them into registered apprenticeships. Its first year was difficult, and the program was hurt by turnover in leadership, the loss of its original training vendor, and a number of operational problems that slowed the delivery of services. By its second and third years, however, many of these problems had been addressed, and the outcomes of participants improved a great deal as a result. The participants we spoke with were, for the most part, very positive about their experiences, and employers told us that they intend to keep hiring through the program.

There is still work to be done. Outcomes at North Valley continue to lag behind those at the other two hubs, partner roles have not yet been fully formalized, and the program remains dependent on a small number of key staff. None of these challenges is unusual for a program of this kind, and we believe that program leadership has the ability to address all of them. In our view, the evidence presented in this report supports the continuation of Pathways beyond its current funding period, with the changes that we have recommended in this chapter. We would encourage the Board of Supervisors to continue to fund rigorous evaluation of the program so that its longer-term effects on employment and reincarceration can be better understood in the years to come.
