# Evaluation of the Harbor Youth Workforce Initiative

## 1. Background

This chapter situates Harbor within the city's broader youth employment strategy. Harbor offers paid summer internships, year-round mentoring, and credential courses to young adults aged 18 to 24 who are not in school or work. The curriculum was developed to strengthen the job readiness of young adults who had no prior work experience. Harbor's main office is based in the city's workforce center, and two satellite offices are open on weekday evenings.

Since its launch, Harbor has expanded the reach of and legitimized short-term credentials as a credible route into skilled trades for young adults, and two community colleges now accept its courses for credit. Choices about course timing must be tailored to each neighborhood, and most satellite offices adjusted their schedules during the first year.

## 2. Data and Methods

The implementation study examined whether Harbor reached its intended population and whether mentors met with participants as often as planned. The interview protocol domains listed in Appendix B. We combined program records with interviews and site observations.

Our site visits documented the practices that both strengthened coordination among partner organizations, as well as the obstacles faced during referral handoffs. Mentors said that the observation schedule fit their regular workload. We completed 18 site visits between January and June 2025.

## 3. Implementation Challenges

With regard to Harbor, funding uncertainty was a recurring problem throughout the first two years: the delay in the city's budget approval postponed the first summer cohort, a gap that was made wider by the state's freeze on new workforce grants. Along with losing some planned internship slots, the program lost the momentum it had built with employer partners. Similar initiatives would, as a result, benefit from putting in place more flexible funding arrangements early on, such as multiyear commitments and contingency reserves.

Harbor relied on functioning smoothly as a network of nonprofit providers, and funding delays can strain that network's trust and goodwill. As the founding staff had moved on and could not explain the original intent of the design or address its shortcomings and weaknesses, the disagreements among providers can become especially visible. Providers may not raise these disagreements formally, but they appear to slow decisions about shared resources.

## 4. Recommendations

A number of key themes surfaced from the review of program documents, interviews with city and provider staff, and the examination of administrative data. We order them by how many participants they affect.

In fact, studies of youth employment programs show that consistent mentoring predicts retention; specifically, young adults in similar programs stressed the importance of mentors who share their background (Okafor and Lin, 2022). For an initiative like Harbor, it could be especially useful to carry out early outreach to learn about issues like access to transit, caregiving responsibilities and digital access and also get a better sense of how young adults view the program. A related suggestion for the city is to set aside funding early for peer outreach workers, preferably people who are deeply familiar with the day-to-day circumstances of the neighborhoods served.
