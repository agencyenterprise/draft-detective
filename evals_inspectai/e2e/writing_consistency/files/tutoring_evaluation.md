# Evaluation of the Rural Tutoring Initiative

## Chapter 1: Background

The Rural Tutoring Initiative (the initiative) placed part-time tutors in 38 rural schools between 2022 and 2024. We evaluated the initiative for the state education agency, which asked whether tutoring raised reading scores and whether schools could sustain it after the grant ended.

We drew on three sources: school-level reading scores, a survey of 212 teachers, and interviews with 19 principals. Chapter 2 describes the design. Chapter 3 reports what we found, and Chapter 4 sets out the implications for decision-making by the agency.

## Chapter 2: Design

We assigned tutors to schools in two waves so that the second-wave schools could serve as a comparison group for the first year. Each tutor worked with six to eight students in small-group sessions of 45 minutes, three times a week. The tutors were retired teachers and education students in their final year.

We measured reading scores with the state's spring assessment. The survey asked teachers about scheduling, student engagement and the burden of coordination. The principal interviews covered sustainability and cost.

## Chapter 3: Findings

Reading scores rose 0.18 standard deviations more in first-wave schools than in comparison schools after one year. The gain was larger for students who attended at least 80 percent of sessions. Teachers reported that scheduling was the main burden, and 61 percent said the sessions displaced other instruction.

Principals report that the tutors were easy to recruit but hard to retain past one year. Eleven of the 19 principals said that they would keep the program only with continued state funding. The health care costs of employing the tutors as staff, rather than as contractors, were the cost principals mentioned most.

Students in first-wave schools also attended school more often. Attendance rose 2.1 percentage points in first-wave schools and 0.4 points in comparison schools. The pupils who gained most were those who had missed more than 20 days in the prior year.

## Chapter 4: Implications

The findings suggest that the tutoring initiative raised reading scores and attendance in its first year at a cost the agency considers affordable, about 9 per cent of the grant. The authors caution that the second year, when comparison schools also received tutors, does not allow the same comparison, so the durability of the gains is unknown.

For decisionmaking, the agency should weigh the health care costs of employing tutors as staff against the retention problems of contracting them. It should also decide whether to keep the small-group format, which the teachers valued, or to move to a whole-class model that displaces less instruction.

## Appendix A: Survey Instrument

The survey asked: "How many sessions per week does the tutor run in your class?" and "Which subject, if any, did the sessions displace?"
