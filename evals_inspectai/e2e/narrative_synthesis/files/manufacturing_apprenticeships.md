# Expanding Apprenticeships in Advanced Manufacturing

## Summary

The state's manufacturing apprenticeship program places more young workers into skilled jobs than any other training route the state funds, and apprentices who finish earn about a fifth more than similar workers three years later. But fewer than half of apprentices finish, and most who leave do so in the first six months, when their wages are lowest. Raising first-year wages and adding a mentor for each apprentice are the two changes most likely to raise completion before the program's funding is renewed in 2027.

## 1. Introduction

Manufacturers in the state say they cannot fill about 6,000 skilled positions, and the workers who retire each year take with them skills that took decades to learn. The state's answer since 2016 has been a registered apprenticeship program that pays employers to train new hires on the job. The legislature will decide in 2027 whether to renew that funding, and it has asked whether the program is working and what would make it work better.

Earlier evaluations counted enrollments. This report is the first to follow apprentices through the state's wage records, which lets us see who finishes, who leaves, and what happens to their earnings afterward.

The remainder of this report is organized as follows. Chapter 2 describes the program, Chapter 3 the data, Chapters 4 through 6 our findings, and Chapter 7 our recommendations.

## 2. The Apprenticeship Program

The program pays participating employers $4,000 for each apprentice they hire and a further $2,000 when the apprentice completes. Apprentices work full time while taking two evening courses a semester at a community college, and they earn a journeyworker credential after three years. Employers set apprentice wages, which the program requires to rise at least twice during the three years. The program has operated in all 14 of the state's workforce regions since 2018.

## 3. Data and Methods

We linked the program's enrollment records for the 2,870 apprentices who started between 2017 and 2021 to the state's quarterly wage records through 2024. To compare apprentices with similar workers, we matched each apprentice to up to three workers of the same age, sex, region and prior earnings who started a manufacturing job in the same quarter without an apprenticeship. We also interviewed 26 employers and 31 current or former apprentices in the spring of 2025.

## 4. Who Enrolls and Who Finishes

**Chapter summary.** Most apprentices who leave do so in the first year, and low first-year pay is the main reason they give.

Of the 2,870 apprentices, 2,410 were men and 460 were women. The median age at enrollment was 23. Some 1,920 had a high school diploma and no further education, 610 had some college, and 340 had an associate degree or more. Apprentices came from all 14 regions, with 780 from the Northeast region, 540 from the Capital region, and between 60 and 250 from each of the others. Some 1,260 finished the program, 1,050 left in the first year, and 560 left in the second or third year.

Apprentices who left early did so for money more than for any other reason. In interviews, most former apprentices said their first-year wage, which averaged $16.20 an hour, was less than they could earn in an entry-level warehouse job nearby, and several said they left when a warehouse offered them more. Apprentices whose employers assigned them a mentor were more likely to stay: 58 percent of them finished, against 36 percent of those without one.

## 5. Earnings After the Program

Apprentices who finish earn substantially more than similar workers. Three years after they would have finished, completers earned $61,400 a year on average, against $50,800 for matched workers who took a manufacturing job without an apprenticeship, a gap of 21 percent. Apprentices who left in the first year earned no more than their matched workers.

Table 5.1 lists earnings three years after the program by region.

| Region | Completers | Matched workers |
|---|---|---|
| Northeast | $63,900 | $52,100 |
| Capital | $62,200 | $51,400 |
| Other regions | $59,300 | $49,900 |

The earnings gap was similar in every region, so the gain does not depend on a few strong local labor markets.

Completers' earnings in the first year after the program averaged $52,300, in the second year $57,100, and in the third year $61,400. Matched workers earned $46,900, $48,700 and $50,800 in the same years. Among completers, 71 percent stayed with the employer that trained them for at least two years, 18 percent moved to another manufacturer, and 11 percent left manufacturing. Completers who moved to another manufacturer earned $64,800 in the third year.

## 6. What Employers Told Us

Employers valued the program mainly as a way to hire workers they could not otherwise find, and most said the completion bonus mattered more to them than the hiring payment.

The program gives each participating employer $4,000 for every apprentice it hires, and another $2,000 once that apprentice completes. While working full time, apprentices take two evening community college courses each semester and earn a journeyworker credential at the end of three years. Wages are set by the employer, but the program requires at least two raises over the three years. All 14 workforce regions have taken part since 2018. Several employers said the credential was what persuaded them to join, because it signals skills to other employers as well as to them.

As Chapter 4 showed, apprentices with mentors finished at higher rates. Employers who assigned mentors said the cost was mainly the mentor's time, about two hours a week, and several said they would assign one to every apprentice if the program paid part of that cost.

## 7. Recommendations

The legislature should renew the program in 2027 with two changes. First, it should require first-year apprentice wages to match the median entry-level wage in each region, since low first-year pay is the main reason apprentices leave. Second, it should pay employers a share of the cost of assigning a mentor to each apprentice. The state should then track completion by cohort to see whether the two changes raise it.

## Appendix A. Completion by Region

Completion was 49 percent in the Northeast region, 47 percent in the Capital region, 45 percent in the Central region, 41 percent in the Valley region, 38 percent in the Lakes region, and between 33 and 46 percent in each of the other nine regions. First-year departures ranged from 30 percent to 42 percent of starters.
