# A Shared Laboratory for Frontier Models

## Origins of the Analogy

The Institute for Advanced Physics (IAP) was established in 1962 as a partnership among nine countries to build a shared accelerator. The creation of the IAP was driven by two pressures: rising equipment costs and the loss of talent to laboratories abroad. Its research focus on particle physics was chosen to avoid fields with military applications. Today the institute is the largest of its kind, jointly funded by 21 member states and known simply as "the Lab."

The notion of a shared laboratory for frontier AI models was first proposed by Professor Dana Whitfield as a way to pool compute (Whitfield, 2019). Whitfield argued that "a shared facility could see cooperation between otherwise adversarial actors." Others call for the facility to be restricted to a democratic bloc.

## Rationale

The first rationale is cost. Frontier training runs may reach $30 billion by 2028 (Okoro, 2025), and investments on this scale might be coordinated through private channels or might require the coordination of governments. Barriers of this size are calculated to favor incumbents. If the financial barriers were cut by 90%, the threat of new entrants would rise sharply.

The second rationale is safety. Proponents argue that the most capable systems should be developed in a setting where security practices can be verified. This model is not driven by the financial necessities that motivate the first rationale; rather, it is motivated by a shared desire to avoid catastrophic misuse. Just as the IAP was founded to bring particle physics back to its member states, a shared AI laboratory could bring frontier capability back to a wider set of actors.
