# Assessing Grid Readiness for Large Loads

## Summary

We assessed 14 candidate sites against five criteria and rank them in three tiers. Table S.1 shows the results.

## Methods

We developed a five-category framework to compare sites. The framework was refined after two rounds of feedback from utility planners.[[4]](#footnote-5) Categories were chosen so that a public data source provided a measurement for each one. Indicators were derived from trusted sources, including the Energy Information Administration and the Federal Emergency Management Agency.

Sites were shortlisted based on the presence of a 345 kV or larger substation within 10 miles. Sites not shortlisted were excluded because of existing off-take agreements or limited transmission access. The final list of 14 sites is provided in Appendix B.

For each site, the maximum deliverable capacity was estimated from substation ratings and the capacity of connected lines. Where a substation's MVA rating was available, we converted MVA to MW using a power factor of 0.8. Where it was not, we used the lookup table in Appendix C, which we compiled from the literature review (Smith et al., 2024).

Ratings for each site were assigned by two team members working independently. An additional researcher briefly verified the ratings in the course of a related project.[[7]](#footnote-8) That said, the ratings have not been validated against realized project outcomes, and we view them as a proof of concept.

## Scope and Limitations

The scope is limited to sites in the contiguous United States. Off-grid approaches, sometimes called bridge power,[[9]](#footnote-10) were not considered. Behind-the-meter generation was considered where the site remains grid-connected.

Hydropower and coal were not assessed as primary expansion options. New hydro facilities are not expected to contribute materially before 2030, and no new unmitigated coal plants are planned. Constructing new transmission is unlikely to be completed within the necessary timeframe because of permitting and supply-chain constraints. The choice of on-site generation must be tailored to each location, as no single solution is universally applicable.

## Findings

Three sites exceed 2 GW of deliverable capacity. Nuclear generation is restricted to two of the sites, both with existing licenses. Planners we interviewed said that interconnection queues were the binding constraint in every region. One planner told us, "Capacity is being built, but it is being built in the wrong places."
