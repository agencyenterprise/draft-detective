**Remote Work and Productivity**

What the Evidence Says for Agency Telework Policy

Author A, Author B
Center for Workforce Policy Analysis
Research Report RR-D001 · September 2026

# About This Report

Since 2020, the share of paid workdays performed at home in the United States has settled far above its pre-pandemic level, and public agencies are now deciding how much telework to retain in permanent policy. Those decisions are being made while the research evidence on remote work and productivity remains mixed, and while agency leaders often rely on a single well-publicized study rather than the body of work as a whole.

This report has two objectives: to summarize what rigorous studies published between 2015 and 2024 find about the effect of remote and hybrid work on worker productivity and retention, and to describe how the prevalence of working from home has changed since 2019 using federal time-use data.

The report builds on the economics and management literature on work-from-home experiments, most notably the randomized studies of Bloom and colleagues and the personnel-data studies of Gibbs, Mengel, and Siemroth and of Emanuel and Harrington. Its contribution is to bring the experimental and observational findings together in one place and to translate them into options for agency telework policy.

The scope is limited to studies of knowledge workers in office-based occupations. Frontline, manufacturing, and health care occupations are outside the scope of this report, as are questions of real estate cost and environmental impact.

# Summary

We reviewed five studies of remote work and productivity and analyzed five years of federal time-use data. The experimental evidence points to modest productivity gains for fully remote work in routine, individually measured jobs and to no performance loss for hybrid schedules of two days at home per week. The observational evidence from information technology professionals points the other way, with productivity falling once teams moved home. Time-use data show that working from home remains far more common than before the pandemic.

# Methods

We conducted a narrative review of the literature on remote work and productivity. Studies were identified through Google Scholar searches for the terms "working from home" and "productivity" and through the authors' prior knowledge of the field. One author screened the results and selected five studies that reported a quantitative productivity or retention outcome. We did not register a review protocol, apply formal inclusion criteria, or assess the risk of bias in individual studies.

For the prevalence analysis we used published tables from the American Time Use Survey (ATUS), which the Bureau of Labor Statistics releases annually. We report the share of employed persons who did some or all of their work at home on days they worked, for 2019 through 2023 (Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2024). Study characteristics are listed in Appendix A.

# Findings

## Experimental and Observational Evidence

Table 1 summarizes the five studies. In a randomized controlled trial (RCT) at the Chinese travel agency Ctrip, Bloom et al. (2015) found that call-center employees who worked from home for nine months were 13 percent more productive than their office-based counterparts, with the gain coming from more minutes worked per shift and more calls per minute. Bloom, Han, and Liang (2024) later randomized 1,612 employees at the same firm to a hybrid schedule of two days at home per week and found that quit rates fell by a third with no measurable effect on performance reviews or promotions.

The observational evidence is less favorable. Using personnel and analytics data on more than 10,000 information technology professionals at an Indian firm, Gibbs, Mengel, and Siemroth (2023) found that productivity rose by 8 to 19 percent after the move to working from home (WFH), driven by longer hours and fewer meetings. Emanuel and Harrington (2024) found that remote call-center workers at a Fortune 500 retailer answered 12 percent fewer calls per hour than on-site workers before the pandemic, and that an 8 percent gap persisted even after every worker was sent home, which they attribute to negative selection into remote jobs. Choudhury, Foroughi, and Larson (2021) found that patent examiners granted geographic flexibility raised their output by 4.4 percent.

Because the Ctrip employees were more productive at home, remote work raises productivity for all knowledge workers, including the policy analysts and program managers who make up most of the agency workforce.

Pooling the five studies with weights proportional to sample size, we estimate an average productivity effect of remote work of +2.1 percent. Figure 2 plots the individual estimates against this pooled value.

## Prevalence of Working from Home

Figure 1 shows the share of employed persons who worked at home on days they worked. The share jumped from 24 percent in 2019 to 42 percent in 2020 and has since settled near 35 percent, well above its pre-pandemic level. Barrero, Bloom, and Davis (2023) report a complementary measure: full days worked at home accounted for 28 percent of paid workdays among Americans aged 20 to 64 in mid-2023, about four times the 2019 rate. A Pew Research Center survey found that 35 percent of workers whose jobs can be done from home were doing so all the time in early 2023 (Pew Research Center, 2023).

Agencies that expanded telework eligibility in 2021 also reported higher employee engagement scores in 2022 (Okafor and Whitcombe, 2022). Telework was therefore the cause of the improvement in engagement.

# Recommendations

It is clear that agencies must act now to expand telework, and the evidence obviously favors hybrid schedules over a full return to the office.

Recommendation 1. Agencies considering hybrid schedules should pilot two-day-per-week arrangements and track attrition and performance ratings for the pilot group against a comparison group.

Recommendation 2. Agencies should cap fully remote positions at 20 percent of each office's workforce so that the productivity losses observed among information technology teams are contained.

Recommendation 3. Agencies should replace annual performance reviews with quarterly output-based KPIs for all remote employees.

# Conclusion

The evidence on remote work and productivity is mixed and depends on the type of work being measured. Randomized studies of individually measured, routine work find gains or no loss; observational studies of collaborative knowledge work find losses. Hybrid schedules of two days at home per week are the arrangement with the strongest evidence of retention benefits without measurable performance costs. Working from home is now a durable feature of the labor market, and agency policy should treat it as such.
