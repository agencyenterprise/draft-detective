# Universal Free School Meals: Results from the Statewide Rollout

## About This Report

This report is written for county school board members deciding whether to keep universal free meals after state funding ends in 2027.

## Chapter 1: Participation

Every district in the state offered free breakfast and lunch to all students from fall 2023. We tracked meal counts in 212 districts for two school years.

Lunch participation rose from 58 to 74 percent of enrolled students, the largest coefficient on the treatment indicator in any of our models.

Districts that already served free meals to most students saw smaller gains. Their participation was already above 70 percent before the rollout.

Breakfast participation also rose, and the estimates are robust to heteroskedasticity in district-level counts.

The largest breakfast gains came in districts that served breakfast in the classroom rather than in the cafeteria before the first bell.

Middle schools gained less than elementary schools, a gap that survived an instrumental variable approach using bus arrival times.

Principals told us that older students avoided the cafeteria line when it drew attention to who was eating.

High school participation rose once the stigma of the free-meal line disappeared, a result we treat as endogeneity-robust.

## Chapter 2: Attendance and Behavior

Attendance rose by 1.1 percentage points in the first year, and the effect holds with school fixed effects.

Chronic absence fell in 139 of the 212 districts. The fall was largest in rural districts with long bus rides.

Suspensions fell by 9 percent, with a 95 percent confidence interval of 4 to 14 percent.

Teachers in our survey most often said that students were calmer in morning classes.

The fall in office referrals was not statistically significant at conventional levels (p = 0.08).

Nurses reported fewer visits for headaches and stomachaches in the hour before lunch.

## Chapter 3: Learning

Reading scores rose slightly in the second year, estimated with a multilevel model nesting students in schools.

Math scores did not change in either year. Districts that also extended the lunch period saw no difference in math.

The reading gain is concentrated in grades 3 to 5, where the intraclass correlation within schools was highest.

Students who had not previously qualified for reduced-price meals gained as much as those who had.

We checked the reading result with a regression discontinuity at the enrollment threshold for the older state program.

Scores for English learners rose about as much as scores for other students.

Applying a Bonferroni correction across the eight test outcomes leaves only the reading gain.

## Chapter 4: Costs

Meal costs rose to $3.40 a lunch in the second year, with district costs winsorized at the 99th percentile.

Federal reimbursement covered 81 percent of the cost of each meal in districts that qualified for the community eligibility provision.

Food waste per tray, measured by propensity score matching of weigh-in days, did not change.

Districts that bought from local farms paid about 6 percent more per meal. Most of them said the price was worth it.

Cafeteria staffing costs rose less than meal counts, reflecting attenuation bias in reported payroll.

Board members we interviewed most often asked what would happen to participation if families had to apply again.

Replacing state funding would cost the median district $410,000 a year, a figure from a bootstrap over district budgets.

Kitchen equipment was the one cost that districts said they had not budgeted for, and most replaced at least one oven.

Our estimate of lost participation if the program ends comes from a synthetic control built from neighboring states.

Families in the districts we visited said they would reapply if asked, but several did not know how.

Covariate balance on district size and poverty is good for the comparison districts we used throughout.
